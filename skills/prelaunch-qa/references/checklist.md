# The pre-launch checklist — all 12 items in full

Items 1–10 are the book's `[T p.77–78]`, in the book's order. Items 11–12 are 2026 additions. Each item: what it tests, what good looks like, the fail signal, and the fix shape.

---

## 1. Strong hook

**Tests:** the three golden hook rules — canon §1. Do not restate them here; run them.

**What good looks like.** The first line or first frame does all three jobs at once, and you can point at which words do which. A hook that only calls out the audience fails: "calling out the audience is not enough on its own — give them a reason to pay attention" `[T p.33]`.

**Fail signal.** You have to argue for one of the three. If the benefit is "implied" only to someone who already knows the product, it isn't implied.

**Fix shape.** Name the failing rule and hand to `hook-system`. Do not rewrite the hook inside a QA verdict — a QA pass that rewrites strategy stops being a QA pass.

**Precondition, checked first.** Is the desire proven and large, and the audience unambiguous? Canon §1's two outside gates. A perfect hook on an unproven desire will not scale `[T p.65]`, and no checklist item catches that later.

---

## 2. Visuals speak to the target audience

**Tests:** every visual is tied to what this specific audience wants.

**What good looks like.** For each scene or element, you can say in one clause which audience want it serves. Actors, wardrobe, environment, props and setting all read as *their* world, not a generic one.

**Fail signal.** A visual that exists because it looked good, because it was already shot, or because the brand likes it. Environment mismatched to the persona's actual life. A model who reads as a different buyer than the one named in the hook.

**Fix shape.** Name the offending scene and the audience want it fails to serve. Suggest the substitute environment or subject.

---

## 3. The visuals pop

**Tests:** raw arresting quality. "Visuals that do not pop, do not perform" `[T p.77]`.

**What good looks like** — the book's three levers:
- **High-contrasting colours** — subject separates hard from background.
- **High-saturation scenery** that grabs the eye.
- **Unusual angles, unusual things, unusual places.**

**Fail signal.** Everything mid-tone. Flat lighting. The angle any phone would default to. A composition indistinguishable from the three ads above it in the feed.

**Fix shape.** Name the frame and the lever to pull. "Front-on at eye level in a beige kitchen — shoot low from floor level, or move to the tiled bathroom for the colour break."

**2026 note.** Front-load *recognisable*, not merely arresting. Kantar (5 Feb 2026): Instagram matched TV-level recall thanks to brand cues landing within the first two seconds. Distinctiveness beats novelty — an arresting frame nobody can attribute is a wasted second.

---

## 4. Easy for eyes to flow through the creative

**Tests:** the eye's path is designed, not accidental.

**What good looks like:**
- **Video** — captions **differ in each scene** and are placed where the eyes naturally land for that shot. Caption changes act as the beat marker.
- **Photo** — the hook is easy to see at thumbnail size, and the focus is deliberately directed to one place.

**Fail signal.** The same caption held across three scenes. Caption parked in the safe-zone default while the action is elsewhere. A photo where you can't tell what to read first.

**Fix shape.** Per-scene caption rewrite, or a placement note per shot.

---

## 5. New visuals every ~3 seconds — CRAFT HEURISTIC

**Label this explicitly as craft in every verdict.** It is a pacing standard, not a mechanism claim.

**What good looks like.** Something new enters frame on roughly a 3-second beat — new scene, new subject, new angle, new caption, new object. The book's framing: film and TV refresh about every 10 seconds; social should be faster `[T p.77]`.

**What must not be claimed.** That 3 seconds is when attention is won or lost. `shared/evidence.md`: Meta is deprecating 3-second organic viewer metrics; the evidenced window is shorter and no universal minimum duration threshold exists (IAB/MRC Attention Measurement Guidelines v1.0, Nov 2025).

**The correction that matters.** Kantar (29 Jul 2024, 40 ads, ARF Attention Validation Initiative): **a product introduction at second 7 created new attention.** Attention is re-winnable. A sagging second half is a fixable defect.

**Fix shape.** Timestamp the sag and name what to introduce there — the product, a demonstration, a reveal, a turn in the story. Never fail an ad on this item alone; it goes in CRAFT NOTES, not ISSUES.

---

## 6. Visuals provide belief

**Tests:** canon §4's belief rule — people believe what they see, not what they hear `[T p.40, p.77]`.

**What good looks like.** Identify the single claim the ad most needs believed. Then find the frame that proves it. Product-in-action is the hardest to shoot and the highest belief — canon §4.

**Fail signal.** The load-bearing claim is carried by narration, caption, or on-screen text alone. Testimonial audio over B-roll that shows nothing related. A durability claim with no durability event on screen.

**Fix shape.** Name the claim and the shot that would prove it. If the claim can't be shown, the honest note is that the claim should be cut or the media type changed — which is a `testing-and-iteration` hypothesis (media-type change), not a QA fix.

---

## 7. Not boring

**Tests:** whether you would actually watch it. "Be brutally honest with yourself. If so, it will likely not work" `[T p.77]`.

**How to run it honestly.** Watch it once at full speed, muted, without reading your own script. Then answer out loud: would I have kept watching if I hadn't made this? If the answer needs a qualifier, it's a fail.

**Fail signal.** The ad is *competent* — correct structure, clean edit, nothing wrong — and inert. Competence is the most common form of boring.

**Fix shape.** Name the dead stretch and what it lacks: no tension, no stakes, no surprise, no reason to stay. Boring is usually a concept problem surfacing late; say so and route to `creative-concepting`.

---

## 8. Compare the final ad to the inspo

**Tests:** whether the build kept what made the reference work.

**What good looks like.** Side by side, you can name the mechanism the inspo used and point to where this ad does the same job. Differences are deliberate.

**Fail signal.** The ad copies the reference's *execution* — same format, same shot list — and drops its mechanism. Or something structural was quietly lost in production: the demonstration, the turn, the escalation.

**Fix shape.** Name the missing structural element, not the missing aesthetic.

---

## 9. Send it to a friend

**Tests:** an outside first reaction.

**What good looks like.** Someone with no stake in the build watches it once and says what went through their mind. They need not be advertising experts `[T p.78]` — expertise is not what you're buying. You want the unprompted first thought.

**Fail signal.** Nobody outside the build has seen it. Or the reviewer was briefed first, which destroys the test.

**Fix shape.** Ask one question only: "what did you think that was about?" Confusion in the answer is the finding.

---

## 10. Wait 72 hours and review again

**Tests:** proximity bias.

**What good looks like.** Three days between build and final review `[T p.78]`. In agency reality, a second reviewer who has not touched the asset is an acceptable substitute for the wait.

**Fail signal.** Reviewed only by the person who built it, on the day they built it.

**Fix shape.** Schedule the re-review, or name the second reviewer. If neither is possible before the upload window, say so in the verdict — it is a stated risk, not a silent one.

---

## 11. Claims substantiated — 2026 addition

**Tests:** every factual assertion in shipped copy traces to a source the client supplied.

**What good looks like.** Statistics, percentages, rankings, superlatives, "clinically proven", "studies show", "#1", "most", counts of customers — each traceable to a client document, a verifiable public source, or the client's own account data. Reviews quoted verbatim, never composed.

**Fail signal.** Any number nobody can source. Plausibility is not sourcing. Canon §8: no invented statistics, ever. The book models this badly at `[T p.86]`.

**Fix shape.** Cut the number, or replace it with a demonstration that shows the same thing. A shot of the result outperforms an unsourceable percentage anyway (item 6).

---

## 12. Policy risk flagged — 2026 addition

**Tests:** whether a known high-risk shape is present and has been escalated.

**What good looks like.** Risk named, policy URL cited, human read requested. See `policy-risk-shapes.md`.

**Fail signal.** A high-risk shape present with no flag. Or worse — a compliance opinion stated. This plugin never asserts a policy rule and never clears copy as compliant.

**Fix shape.** The three-step flag. That is the whole deliverable.

---

## The final gate before a Pass

Run these in order. They are the 12 items of `SKILL.md`'s table expressed as a tick-list; items 9 and 10 share a box because either satisfies the outside-eye test.

- [ ] Hook clears all three golden rules; the one it would fail is named if not.
- [ ] Every visual is tied to something this named audience wants.
- [ ] Visuals pop — contrast, saturation, or an angle that isn't expected.
- [ ] Video captions differ per scene and sit in the eye's path; photo hook is instantly findable.
- [ ] The thing that must be believed is **shown**.
- [ ] You would not scroll past it. Said out loud, honestly.
- [ ] Compared to the inspo; the missing structural element is named or confirmed absent.
- [ ] A non-expert has reacted, or a 72-hour gap has passed.
- [ ] Every number traces to a client-supplied source, or it's gone.
- [ ] Policy risk either absent or flagged with the URL and a human-read request.
- [ ] Pacing notes written as craft, with timestamps.

All boxes checked: Pass. Otherwise Needs changes, each issue with the exact element, the rule, and the fix.
