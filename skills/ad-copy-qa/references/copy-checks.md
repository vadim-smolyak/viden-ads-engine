# Copy checks — the seven priority checks (the fast path)

The fast path in full: run these seven, return the verdict, stop. That is the whole job for "check this before I submit it."

**Priority checks are where VIDEN copy actually fails; never skip them.** The other three shards are opt-in:

## Priority checks (never skip)

### 1. Grammar and naturalness
Read every line out loud. Flag anything ungrammatical, awkward, or that no native speaker would say. Watch for the recurring tells: missing articles ("a", "the"), wrong prepositions, slightly-off idioms, tense drift mid-sentence, and every sentence built to the same length.

Always put the corrected line in the Rewrite field. A flag without a fix teaches nothing.

### 2. Empty claims and hollow superlatives
Cut any line that sounds like copy and says nothing. The test: **point at the feature behind the line.** If you cannot, it fails.

- "Designed to stay compact without losing function" — names nothing.
- "The most versatile X ever" — unsubstantiated superlative.
- "Elevate your everyday" — no referent.

Replace with the mechanism: the named part, the named number that exists in the brand artefact, the named moment.

### 3. Claim substantiation
Canon §8 owns the rule: **no invented statistics, ever.** This check enforces it on the page.

What counts as an invented statistic:
- Any percentage, multiple, ranking or count not present in the brand artefact or supplied by the client for this ad.
- "9 out of 10 X" style constructions. The book itself models this badly at `[T p.86]` — do not follow it there.
- "Most", "#1", "the leading", "clinically proven", "dermatologist recommended" without the artefact carrying the substantiation.
- Aggregate review counts and star averages that were true last quarter. Numbers age; re-check against the artefact.

The fix is one of two things and never a third: **swap in a real figure from the artefact**, or **cut the number and make the claim qualitative.** Never hedge an invented number into "up to" or "as much as" — that is the same fabrication with a softener.

### 4. Match the asset
The copy describes the product, variant, colour, size, material and scene that are actually shown. If the ad shows one thing and the copy says another, it fails, however good the line is.

Also check the direction the asset implies. A hook promising a shared experience over a visual of one person alone is the failure the book diagnoses in its $10,000 case study `[T p.66]` — audience blur plus hook/visual mismatch.

### 5. Hook present and passing
The first line of the primary text (and the headline, where the headline is the scroll-stopper) must clear all three golden rules — canon §1. Do not restate the rules here; run the gate and report which of the three failed.

A hook that fails the gate is not a note in the verdict. It is a rewrite, and the rewrite is the deliverable. Reach for `hook-system` for the tactic and phrasing libraries if the failure is structural rather than a wording slip.

### 6. Simple, not clever
Canon §8 owns the standard: simple beats clever, always `[T p.72]`. Clever copy asks the reader to solve a puzzle before they get the point, and the feed does not grant that time.

Flags: a pun in the first line; a headline whose meaning only lands after the body copy; wordplay that depends on knowing the product; a metaphor stacked on a metaphor; any line the writer is proud of for its construction rather than its clarity.

### 7. Voice and register consistency
Against the brand artefact, not against taste. Check:
- banned words and phrases, verbatim from the artefact;
- punctuation habits (em dashes, semicolons, trailing ellipses, exclamation policy);
- register — whether the brand's paid copy is editorial, conversational, founder-voiced, or creator-voiced, and whether this draft holds it;
- product naming — full names, capitalisation, and never the shortened form if the artefact bans it;
- register drift inside one ad: an editorial opener that turns into hype by line four.

A quote pulled from the VOC bank may contain a word the brand bans in its own copy. Keep the quote verbatim; do not adopt the word in the lines around it.
