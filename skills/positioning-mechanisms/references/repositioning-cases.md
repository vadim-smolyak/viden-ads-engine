# Positioning cases — the move, and what to copy

Nine cases. Each is situation → move → why it worked → what to copy. Grouped by play so a partial read is useful: new mechanism first (highest leverage), then identity, then superiority, then the deposition.

Every figure here is the book's own reported figure, cited to the page. Do not extrapolate from them, and do not present them to clients as benchmarks.

---

## New mechanism

### 1. Paint-by-numbers → girls' night `[T p.22, p.29–30]`

**Situation.** Client stuck at $150k/month. 45 days and roughly 60 creatives tested with zero success. Nearly churned.

**Move.** Comment mining found two girls tagging each other under an ad: *"Girls night soon?"* The product was repositioned from a craft product to a girls'-night activity. Hook: *"This is for my girlies who need a girl's night soon!"*

**Result as reported.** Immediate hit; roughly doubled revenue for six straight months; peaked at $2.5M revenue in December 2023. The client's 12-month $100k-profit-month goal was hit in under six months.

**Why it worked.** A new mechanism does not require a new product. Card and board games already owned the girls'-night desire; nobody had aimed paint-by-numbers at it. The product was accepted as a viable solution for a desire it was never built for.

**What to copy.** Before reformulating anything, ask which large existing desire this exact product could credibly serve that nobody has aimed it at. Then test whether the market accepts it `[T p.30]`. Read the comments on your own winning ads first — that is where this came from.

### 2. "Emotion-baiting" — naming the vehicle `[T p.29]`

**Situation.** A Facebook-ads offer competing in a market where every strategy had been tried.

**Move.** Coined a mechanism name unique to the agency and put it in the hook: *"How I use emotion-baiting to spend $1,000 per day profitably with Facebook ads."*

**Why it worked.** A named vehicle is unfamiliar, so it is not yet disproven. Reported effects: cheaper clicks, more engaging ads, better cost per purchase, and curiosity specifically from people who had tried everything else.

**What to copy.** Name the mechanism. An unnamed method is a claim; a named method is a thing. The name must describe something the product actually does, or the ad writes a cheque the landing page cannot cash.

## Reposition

### 3. Wall art → gifting `[T p.31, p.92]`

**Situation.** Wall-art brand stuck at $30k/month. Positioning: "hey, this will make your wall look great." Creative was competent.

**Diagnosis.** The positioning had **no urgency**. Nothing about a nicer wall has to happen this week.

**Move.** Research surfaced gifting. Hook: *"When was the last time you surprised your sweetheart?"* All-new content built around it.

**Result as reported.** Past $300k/month.

**Why it worked.** The question makes the buyer realise it has been a while — urgency manufactured from a true observation rather than a deadline. And note what else changed: **the reposition changed the ideal customer.** The buyer became someone shopping for another person.

**What to copy.** When performance stalls with technically fine creative, test the positioning for urgency before touching production. Expect the persona to move with the angle, and rebuild the matrix rather than reusing it. Urgency comparison worth holding onto: "snoring" versus "not being able to sleep in the same bed as my wife because of my snoring" `[T p.92]`.

## Identity

### 4. Black Rifle Coffee `[T p.9]`

**Situation.** Coffee — a commodity where the product cannot be meaningfully superior.

**Move.** Military branding as the whole proposition. Camo and flag packaging, "Freedom Roast" and "Liberty Roast", military-scenario visuals.

**Why it worked.** Not a superior product; a superior *brand identity*. Reported valuation above $1B.

**What to copy.** In a commodity category, stop looking for a product advantage and find a sub-community whose shared interest is strong enough to create demand on its own. The identity has to be real and specific — the packaging, the names and the visuals all had to agree.

### 5. Gymshark `[T p.10]`

**Situation.** Sportswear, dominated by generalised global sports brands.

**Move.** The first gym-wear brand — a category narrowed from "sport" to "gym".

**Why it worked.** Founded 2012 by Ben Francis at 19; reported $1.45B. Narrowing the category was the entire play, and it opened room for a whole tier of sub-community brands underneath it: Alphalete, Inaka, ASRV, BuffBunny, YoungLA.

**What to copy.** Ask what the category would be if it were named after the customer's actual activity rather than the industry's word for it.

### 6. Niching to raise the price `[T p.10]`

**Situation.** A generic "Facebook Ads Inner Circle" offer.

**Move.** Renamed to "Facebook Ads Inner Circle **For Shopify Store Owners Doing At Least $10k per month**".

**Why it worked.** The qualifier in the name does the targeting and justifies a higher price.

**What to copy.** Put the qualifier in the product name, not only in the ad. This is also the mechanism behind the high-AOV persona shift — the agency moved from clients who could barely afford $500/month to $10,000+/month by changing the persona to businesses already doing $100,000+/month, because it is easier to make them more money `[T p.84]`.

## Superiority

### 7. Rose Skin Co. — the hate list `[T p.11]`

**Situation.** At-home IPL, competing with an entrenched set of direct and indirect solutions.

**Move.** Enumerated what people hate about **each** existing solution, then mapped product benefits onto those hatreds.

| Existing solution | What people hate |
|---|---|
| Shaving | Razor burn, weekly time, blade cost, awkward shower positions |
| Professional laser | Exposure to a stranger, drive time, scheduling, per-appointment cost |

Resulting claims: "30 minutes a week, from home, pain-free, zero ingrown hairs."

**Why it worked.** "These benefits mainly focus on what people hate about other solutions in the marketplace. This is what creates our perception of a superior product" `[T p.11]`.

**What to copy.** Do the enumeration per solution, not once for the whole market. Different hatreds produce different benefit claims and different personas. Also note the seasonal layer: the brand re-positions every summer to match the buyer's current state `[T p.92]`.

### 8. iPhone — superiority on three axes `[T p.28]`

**Situation.** An installed base that has already tried the product and its predecessors.

**Move.** Each launch positions on screen size, battery life and camera quality. Only those three.

**Why it worked.** "That is all people desire." Leveraging an existing user base avoids the cost of an educational campaign.

**What to copy.** When the market has already used the category, do not educate — improve the two or three axes they already care about and say only that. Where the product is *not* better, switch to the character-trait niches inside the market instead: gamers, vloggers, business owners, influencers, athletes `[T p.28]`.

## Deposition

### 9. Media buyer → systems builder `[T p.31]`

**Situation.** The author was known as a media-buying expert. Platforms simplified media buying, commoditising the skill he was known for.

**Move.** Deliberately shed that image and repositioned around systems for building better ads.

**Result as reported.** A new market captured: a 100+ member mentorship at up to $1,000/month each, agency retainer moved from $5k to $10k/month, seven figures in 2023.

**Why it worked.** The trigger was correctly read — **your mechanism is at the end of its trend.** Depositioning is giving up recognition you already earned, which is why it is rarely done in time.

**What to copy.** Ask annually whether the thing the brand is known for is still scarce. If the mechanism is being automated, standardised or given away by the platforms, the recognition attached to it is depreciating. Reposition on the next mechanism while the current one still has value.
