# Creative Mechanics — the eight structural moves

A mechanic is the cognitive or emotional move the **whole concept** makes: how the viewer arrives at the truth. Canon §5 separates it from a tactic (the frame of the opening line) and a format (the production vessel). **The mechanic travels; the format is only the vessel** — Implied Answer runs equally as a lifestyle video, a static, a talking head or a grid.

Eight mechanics. Nothing else is a mechanic. If a concept needs a name that is not on this list, it is a tactic, a format or a trigger — find it in the right library instead of inventing a ninth mechanic.

**Repair recorded so nobody re-introduces it: undefined mechanic names dropped.** "Proof Stack" (cited 15 times), "Sensory Proof" (once) and "Pattern Interrupt" *as a mechanic* were never defined in any eight-mechanic library. Those formats are re-pointed to mechanics that exist, or carry no pairing at all. Never carry an undefined mechanic name forward. Pattern Interrupt remains a **format** (`formats-either.md`) and a hook-layer trigger only — canon §5.

**One-line definitions of all eight are in `SKILL.md` Step 5.** Load this file for the structure beats, the worked examples, the layering pairings and the add-a-mechanic protocol — not to look up what a mechanic is.

**Selection table — start here.**

| To make the viewer… | Mechanic |
|---|---|
| Conclude the truth themselves from what they see | Implied Answer |
| Believe a result because someone else noticed it | Social Witness |
| Drop the ad filter entirely | Overheard Conversation |
| Think "I never looked at it that way" | Reframe |

| Attach the product to something they already want | This and a… |

## The Implied Answer

**What it is.** The hook poses a question that sounds like mild judgment, confusion or idle curiosity. The visuals answer it silently with an aspirational truth. Nobody says the answer; the viewer supplies it.

**Why it works.** A self-conclusion bypasses ad scepticism. When the viewer joins the dots, the insight registers as their own thought rather than a brand claim.

**Awareness fit.** Unaware, problem-aware. The question meets them where they are; the visual shows where they could be.

**Structure.**
- Hook: a question someone would plausibly ask *about* the customer.
- Visual: the silent answer — the product's world, unexplained.
- The brand never states the connection.

**Example.** Hook: *"Why do you always stay home?"* Visual: a man hanging extraordinary artwork through a beautiful apartment. Implied truth: his home is so good he does not need to leave. Category: art and home decor.

**More by category.** Sleep — *"Why does she never want to go out any more?"* over an immaculate bedroom. Home gym — *"Why does he never go to the gym?"* over a serious home setup mid-workout. Coffee — *"Why is she always up before everyone?"* over a quiet morning ritual.

## The Social Witness

**What it is.** Someone other than the customer notices the change — a compliment, a double-take, an unsolicited "what are you doing differently?". Impact is validated by a third party's reaction, not by a claim, a review or a before/after.

**Why it works.** Third-party validation outranks self-reported results, and it implies the change is visible enough for other people to register — which raises the cost of not having the product.

**Awareness fit.** Problem-aware, product-aware. Best when the prospect already owns the problem and needs to believe the product works.

**Structure.**
- Setup: the customer living normally, not discussing the product.
- Witness beat: somebody else notices and says so.
- Reaction: low-key. Not a pitch.
- The product appears as the obvious explanation.

**Example.** Hair-growth serum. A woman at work; a colleague stops her in the corridor: *"Wait — did you do something different? Your hair looks incredible."* She just smiles. Product on screen at the end, no voice-over.

**Note.** Frequently a *secondary* mechanic layered onto another. The artwork concept above runs Implied Answer as primary and Social Witness underneath — the question implies somebody noticed the behaviour change.

## The Overheard Conversation

**What it is.** The ad is framed as something the viewer was not meant to see: a text thread, a DM, a group chat, a conversation between friends. It reads as eavesdropping.

**Why it works.** It removes the "this is an ad" filter before it engages. Viewers process it as social content, and claims feel more credible because friends do not use marketing language on each other.

**Awareness fit.** Unaware, problem-aware. Mimics how people actually find products — through other people, not ads.

**Structure.**
- Frame: thread, DM, comment section or overheard dialogue.
- Trigger: one person names a problem or asks a question.
- Resolution: the other casually recommends the product.
- No brand voice-over, no formal CTA. It ends the way a real exchange ends.

**Example.** Gut-health supplement. Opens on a message thread. *"ok don't judge me but I've been using this probiotic."* — *"omg which one"* — a screenshot of the product. End.

## The Reframe

**What it is.** Opens by validating a belief the prospect already holds — something they were told, something they tried, something that makes logical sense — then flips the frame entirely. The product is not the hero. The new perspective is.

**Why it works.** People share the things that changed how they think, not the things that sold them something. The Reframe manufactures the "I never looked at it that way" beat, which drives both conversion and organic sharing.

**Not the Contrarian tactic.** A Contrarian hook flips a belief in the opening line. The Reframe builds to the flip as the architecture of the entire concept. Canon §5.

**Awareness fit.** Problem-aware, solution-aware. Best when they have already tried things that failed and need a new explanation for why.

**Structure.**
- Open: validate the conventional wisdom — "you have been told X".
- Middle: show them doing everything right by that logic.
- Flip: the real problem was something else.
- Resolve: the product solves the *real* problem, not the assumed one.

**Example.** Sleep tracking. Open: *"You've been told you just need eight hours."* Middle: the person doing it all correctly — in bed on time, no screens. Flip: *"The problem was never the hours. It was the quality."* Product: the thing that finally explains why they are still exhausted.

## The Borrowed Enemy

**What it is.** The ad describes a problem, an ingredient, a feeling or an experience that is *obviously* caused by one specific competitor — without ever naming them. The viewer makes the identification.

**Why it works.** Naming a competitor triggers defensiveness, brand-safety objections and policy risk. Making the reference unmistakable without naming it lets the viewer do the conquesting, and a self-conclusion lands harder than a stated claim.

**Awareness fit.** Solution-aware, product-aware. Most effective on people already shopping who have probably used the competitor.

**Structure.**
- Describe the problem in vivid, specific, recognisable detail.
- Use language that could *only* apply to that competitor — a named-in-all-but-name ingredient, form factor, side effect or sensation.
- Never name them.
- Position the product as the natural alternative.

**Example.** Clean energy drink: *"that jittery crash… ingredients you can't pronounce… wondering what you just put in your body."* Every drinker of the market-leading energy drink knows exactly who that is. The product arrives as the obvious alternative.

## The Trojan Horse

**What it is.** The ad reads as education, entertainment or a personal story until the last fifth, where the product appears as part of the resolution. The viewer is invested before they classify it as an ad.

**Why it works.** Ad avoidance peaks at the moment of recognition. Delaying that moment until the viewer is engaged makes the product feel earned rather than inserted.

**Awareness fit.** Unaware. Strongest on cold audiences who will scroll past an ad but watch something genuinely useful or funny.

**Structure.**
- Open as pure content: tutorial, story, observation, bit.
- Build real value or narrative tension with no product mention.
- The product enters as part of the resolution — present, not announced.
- No hard sell; the content already did the work.

**Example.** Knife brand. Opens as a genuine tutorial on dicing an onion properly. Full technique, actually useful. Two minutes in: *"and obviously it helps that this knife holds an edge."* The product was barely the point, and the viewer just watched a two-minute ad.

## The Contrast Without Comment

**What it is.** Two realities side by side — before and after, with and without, old way and new way — and no editorial telling the viewer which is better. The visual settles it. No voice-over passes judgment.

**Why it works.** Stating the obvious reads as advertising. Showing it and leaving the conclusion open reads as evidence. The absence of editorialising is itself the credibility signal.

**Not the Contrast tactic**, which uses copy to point out a mismatch. This mechanic removes the copy and lets the juxtaposition work alone. Canon §5.

**Awareness fit.** Problem-aware, solution-aware. Needs a gap dramatic enough to speak for itself.

**Structure.**
- Split screen, side by side, or a sequential cut.
- Before: the problem state, shot neutrally.
- After: the solved state, shot neutrally.
- No voice-over, no text verdict. Two realities.

**Example.** Mattress. Split screen, silent. Left: groggy wake-up, snooze, dragging out of bed. Right: waking before the alarm, stretching, rested. Nothing said, everything communicated.

## This and a…

**What it is.** Two things are shown or named together — the product and something aspirational, unexpected or emotionally loaded — and the juxtaposition does all the work. The "and a" implies the product belongs in the same category as something already wanted.

**Why it works.** Meaning is made by association. Placing the product beside something with existing emotional value lets it inherit that value without claiming it. The viewer's brain does the linking, which beats a stated claim.

**Not Contrast Without Comment**, which juxtaposes two *opposing* states. This pairs two *complementary* things to lift the product by proximity.

**Awareness fit.** Unaware, problem-aware. For products that gain from identity and lifestyle association rather than functional explanation.

**Structure.**
- Establish the first element — aspirational, nostalgic or culturally loaded.
- Place the product next to it with almost no connective tissue, just "and a".
- Never explain why they belong together. The viewer feels it.

**Examples.** *"Saturdays and a Buoy in your water bottle"* — the day carries the mood, the product rides it. *"Morning light and a quiet apartment and Loop Earplugs"* — the product joins a coveted aesthetic. *"A good book, an iced coffee, and a flight with Loop Earplugs"* — the product slots into a fantasy scenario.

**Production note.** Strongest as a visual list or slow lifestyle montage where each element gets its own beat. The rhythm of the "and a" structure is part of what makes it read as native rather than authored.

## Layering

Mechanics combine. The strongest concepts run one **primary** mechanic that shapes the architecture, plus one **secondary** that adds a second emotional layer. Never three.

| Pairing | What it produces |
|---|---|
| Implied Answer + Social Witness | The question itself implies somebody noticed the change — the artwork concept |
| Trojan Horse + Reframe | Content that builds to a perspective flip |
| Overheard Conversation + Social Witness | The friend recommendation that doubles as validation |
| Contrast Without Comment + Borrowed Enemy | A visual comparison that identifies the competitor without naming them |

## Adding a mechanic

Nine mechanics is a bigger claim than it looks — this list is deliberately closed. Only propose an addition when an ad in the wild cannot be described by any of the eight. Then supply, in order: what was seen (hook, visual, structure); the cognitive move named — what did the viewer have to *do* to get the point; why it bypassed ad resistance; awareness fit; one example plus two more by category. Anything that fails the "what did the viewer have to do" test is a format or a tactic, not a mechanic.
