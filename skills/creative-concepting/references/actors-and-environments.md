# Actors and Environments — performance levers, not logistics

Casting and location are the two production variables the book treats as performance decisions rather than scheduling ones `[T p.61–64]`. Brief them explicitly. *"Do not cut corners short here"* `[T p.64]`.

**The default failure mode:** one person filming in their bedroom in a lower-income household. It converts sometimes, and not reliably `[T p.61]`. If a concept's brief does not specify actor and environment, this is what gets delivered.

---

## Environments

The environment is everything around the actor. It does three jobs.

| Job | What the environment signals | Test question |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | This person has actually done the thing they are claiming | Would the ICP believe this person has authority *because of where they are standing*? |
| **Perceived value** | Sets the price expectation before the price is seen `[T p.61]` | Does the frame read above or below the product's price point? |
| **Recognition** | The ICP recognises the place as their own world | Would the ICP say "that's my job / my kitchen / my gym"? |

**The supercar-versus-studio case `[T p.61]`.** The author ran the same topic as two videos: one filmed in a recording studio, one filmed inside a supercar. The studio version got roughly 300 views; the supercar version got over 2,000. He attributes the gap to established authority — the environment made the claim credible before a word was spoken.

**The Lee Haight case `[T p.61]`.** Lee Haight, who helps roofing contractors, films standing on a roof at live job sites rather than in an office. A roofer recognises the environment instantly. That is the recognition job, and it beats production polish for that audience.

**Perceived value `[T p.61–62]`.** Filming in a wealthy environment raises perceived value before the price is seen. The men's jeans brand shot in luxury apartments, on the street outside designer stores, and in Dubai vacation locations.

**Briefing rule.** Name the environment in the concept, not on set. "Kitchen" is not a brief. "A cluttered family kitchen at 7am, natural light, cereal boxes on the counter" is.

## Actors

Casting comes back to who the ICP wants to become `[T p.63]`. Three archetypes — test one of each, not three of the same.

| Archetype | Cast | Book case |
|---|---|---|
| **Attracted to** | Someone the ICP finds attractive | Redline Steel used a southern male model. The ICP was conservative women in their mid-to-late forties buying metal crosses, yard ornaments and wall art `[T p.63]` |
| **Wants to look like** | The aspirational version of the ICP | The men's jeans brand cast six-packed, tattooed models to hit the "bad boy" persona men privately want to project `[T p.63–64]` |
| **Looks like** | A near-exact match for the ICP | The mushroom powder case below `[T p.64]` |

### The mushroom-powder actor test `[T p.63]`

Same script, three actors, one per archetype: a muscular fitness guy, a vegan save-the-planet girl, and a rich NYC girl. **The vegan girl beat both of the others decisively** — better resonance with the actual ideal customer.

**Carry the method, not the answer.** Which archetype wins is not predictable from the brief. Run one asset per archetype against the same script and let spend decide. Do not reason your way to a casting winner and then shoot only that one — the outcome in this case was the archetype most teams would have deprioritised.

Note the interaction with canon §6: environment, ethnicity, gender and age are also iteration variables on a proven winner. Casting a new archetype against a *winning* script is an iteration; casting a new archetype against new messaging is a concept. Do not confuse the two — canon §5.

## Casting and environment brief block

Attach this to every concept in the deep path. Every field filled, no defaults.

```
ACTOR
  Archetype:        attracted to / wants to look like / looks like
  Age band:
  Read:             what the ICP should think about this person in 2 seconds
  Wardrobe:
  Delivery:         to camera / voice-over / silent performer
  Non-negotiables:  anything the product requires (hands visible, hair type, skin, build)

ENVIRONMENT
  Location:         specific, not a category
  Time of day / light:
  Signal being bought:  authority / perceived value / recognition
  Set dressing:     the 3 objects that make it read as real
  Do not include:   anything that reads below the price point
```

## Common casting failures

- **Casting the buyer when the ICP wants the aspiration.** Product-dependent — that is exactly why all three archetypes get tested.
- **A studio when the ICP needs recognition.** Polish reads as advertising to a trade audience.
- **A wealthy environment on a low-price product.** Perceived value cuts both ways; it can make cheap look suspicious.
- **Actor and hook mismatch.** The book's $10,000 ad promised a date night and showed a woman alone `[T p.66]` — canon §1 already treats this as a hook-gate failure. Verify at casting, not in QA.
- **One actor across the whole batch.** Wastes the single cheapest iteration variable available (canon §6).

## AI avatars in place of an actor

An AI avatar occupies the actor slot and must satisfy the same three archetype questions — an AI avatar the ICP does not recognise, want, or want to be does not work simply because it is cheap to produce. Visibility of the AI is the variable, not the use of AI; the artefact checklist and the Gen Z flag are in `SKILL.md`, sourced in `shared/evidence.md`. The environment behind an avatar is generated too, so it still has to buy authority, perceived value or recognition on purpose.
