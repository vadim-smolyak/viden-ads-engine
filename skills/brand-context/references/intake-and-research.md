# Intake block and research checklist

Two sections. Section 1 is sent to the user verbatim. Section 2 is worked after they reply.

---

## 1. The intake block — send as one message

Send all of it at once, grouped, exactly this shape. Adapt only the brand name.

> Before I research, seven quick ones — answer in one reply, and skip anything you don't know.
>
> **Basics**
> 1. Brand name?
> 2. Website URL?
> 3. Which product or products are we running paid ads for? List them, or say "all".
> 4. Should this context doc cover the full brand, or focus on one product line?
>
> **What you already know**
> 5. What do you know about this brand and its audience that matters for the work — age, gender, lifestyle, pain points, values?
> 6. Who are their main competitors, if you know?
> 7. Any constraints you're already aware of — claims they can't make, tone limits, regulated category, sensitive audience?
>
> **Creative context**
> 8. Any existing creative or messaging from this brand I should keep in mind?

Then, in one line: "Got it. I'll research the rest and come back with the full context doc."

Rules for this phase:

- One message. Never drip.
- Accept partial answers and move on. Unanswered questions become research targets.
- Do not add questions to this block. Anything else you need, ask after research, when you know what is actually missing.
- Question 7 answers become **confirmed** constraints. Everything you infer later is **inferred**.

---

## 2. Research checklist — 9 points

Work them in order. Each carries its own approach. Stop at the point where you have enough to write the section honestly; more fetching does not improve a labelled inference.

### 2.1 Brand story and origin

- How did the brand start? What problem did the founder set out to solve?
- Is there a founder story or mission driving the brand's identity?
- How long have they existed? Startup, scaling, or established?

**Approach.** Fetch the About page. Search `[brand] founder story` and `[brand] how it started`. Check for an origin story on the product pages too — many brands only tell it there.

### 2.2 Full product catalogue

- What do they sell? What are the key SKUs or lines?
- How are products differentiated from each other — size, use case, formulation, audience?
- What is the hero product or primary revenue driver, if identifiable?
- Are there bundles or subscriptions?

**Approach.** Fetch the shop and collection pages; read the site navigation. Navigation order is usually a revenue ranking. Best-seller badges and "most popular" labels identify the hero without guessing.

### 2.3 Product differentiation and unique mechanism

- What makes the product different from the alternatives?
- Is there a proprietary ingredient, format, technology or method?
- How do they explain the point of difference in their own words?
- What claims do they make, and how do they support them?

**Approach.** Fetch product detail pages plus any "How it works" or FAQ page. Look for clinical claims, certifications and patent language. Quote their phrasing verbatim — this section feeds the mechanism work in `positioning-mechanisms`, and a paraphrase there becomes a new claim.

### 2.4 Competitors

- Who are the three to five closest competitors?
- How does each position itself relative to this brand?
- What does this brand do that they do not, and vice versa?

**Approach.** Search `[brand] vs [competitor]`, `[category] alternatives`, `[brand] competitors`. Check whether the brand names competitors in its own copy — a brand that names a rival has already chosen its positioning. Record names and positioning only; competitor *creative* is `market-research`'s job.

### 2.5 The alternative solution

- What was the customer doing **before** this product existed?
- What is the "old way" this product replaces or improves on?

Often not a competitor at all — a behaviour, a DIY approach, a professional service, or doing nothing.

**Approach.** Read reviews and the brand's own problem-framing language; look for "vs traditional methods" content. This section is the input to the indirect-competitor set and the hate-list method in `positioning-mechanisms`.

### 2.6 Core audiences

- Who does the brand actually speak to on its site and in its ads?
- Are there distinct secondary audiences?
- What signals point to the target — imagery, language, which pain points get surfaced, price point, lifestyle cues?

**Approach.** Read homepage and product-page copy closely; pull live ads through Foreplay; read review language. Combine with what the user gave you in intake. Where the user's belief and the site's signals disagree, record both and flag it — that disagreement is usually the most valuable line in the whole document.

### 2.7 Brand voice and tone

- How does this brand communicate? Clinical, warm, witty, direct, community-driven?
- Which adjectives describe the copy style?
- What vocabulary recurs? What do they visibly avoid? What is the emotional register?
- Does the voice shift between product types or audiences?

**Approach.** Read headlines, CTAs, email pop-ups and FAQ answers — FAQ answers are the least art-directed copy on a site and the most honest sample of voice. Capture two or three short verbatim examples rather than describing the voice abstractly.

### 2.8 Inferred creative constraints

Identify the constraints that would actually affect ad creative:

- Category sensitivities — health claims, before/after restrictions, regulated ingredients, financial products
- Audience constraints — older demographics, religious communities, parents, professional audiences
- Special Ad Category exposure — housing, employment, financial products and services, or issues/elections/politics. If any apply, say so explicitly; it changes how the creative has to be written (`positioning-mechanisms`)
- Tone constraints — buttoned-up, family-safe or community-led brands usually have limits on edginess
- Price-point constraints — high-AOV brands cannot run price-shock tactics; budget brands cannot claim premium positioning
- AI-creative constraints — flag a Gen Z-skewed audience, and any EU targeting, for a human call (`shared/evidence.md`)

**Label each one confirmed or inferred.** Confirmed = stated explicitly, by the user or on the site. Inferred = a reasonable read from category and signals.

Never assert a platform policy rule here. Name the risk shape, point at the policy, and mark it for a human read.

### 2.9 Must-know strategic context

Anything a strategist needs before writing a single hook:

- Unusual purchase behaviour — subscription, bundle-heavy, gifting-heavy, considered purchase
- Seasonality, and whether fulfilment has a deadline behind it (`shared/canon.md` §8 — desire has a clock)
- Strong community or UGC presence
- Notable press or cultural moments
- Anything flagged in intake that has not landed in another section

Keep this section to what changes a creative decision. It is not a place for everything that did not fit elsewhere.
