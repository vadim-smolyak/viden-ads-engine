# Persuasion and influence (13)

The classic influence levers. Use these once you know what the buyer believes — `buyer-psychology.md` tells you the state, these change it. `belief-objection-engineering` owns the sequencing; the mechanisms are here.

### Reciprocity Principle
People feel obliged to return a favour. Give first.
**In an ad:** lead with something genuinely useful — the technique, the diagnosis, the free tool — before the ask. It is what makes an educational or advertorial-style ad convert rather than merely inform.

### Commitment & Consistency
People stay consistent with what they have already committed to, especially publicly.
**In an ad:** ask for the small yes first. A quiz answer, a size selection, a saved item. Each step makes the next one consistent rather than new.

### Authority Bias
People defer to credible expertise. Credentials transfer trust to claims.
**In an ad:** the named practitioner with a stated credential. One of the three ways to overcome a held belief `[T p.82]`, and the strongest in categories where the audience expects gatekeeping.

### Liking / Similarity Bias
People say yes to people they like and to people like them.
**In an ad:** cast for resemblance. This is the third actor archetype — people our ideal customer looks like `[T p.63]` — and it is why the mushroom-powder test was won by the actor who matched the buyer, not the fittest one.

### Unity Principle
Shared identity beats mere similarity. "One of us" is the strongest social lever there is.
**In an ad:** insider language, in-group references, the specific niche name rather than the general audience. Identity marketing `[T p.9]` runs entirely on this.

### Scarcity / Urgency Heuristic
Limited availability raises perceived value and forces a decision now.
**In an ad:** stock counts, closing windows, small batches — stated once and true. False scarcity costs trust permanently, and the book is explicit that an announced price rise must actually happen `[T p.83]`.

### Foot-in-the-Door
A small accepted request makes a larger one more likely.
**In an ad:** the low-commitment first offer that leads somewhere. Sample, trial, starter size.

### Door-in-the-Face
An oversized request first makes the real one look reasonable by contrast.
**In an ad:** show the cost of the premium alternative before your price. Rose Skin Co. selling against thousands in laser appointments is this and Contrast Effect together `[T p.79]`.

### Loss Aversion / Prospect Theory
Losses register as roughly twice as significant as equivalent gains.
**In an ad:** frame around what continues to be lost by doing nothing — the hours, the money, the repeat purchases of the thing that does not work. Do not attach a multiplier to it in copy; it is a finding, not a claim about this ad.

### Anchoring Effect
The first number seen sets the frame for every number after it.
**In an ad:** lead with the comparison price, the original price, or the cost of the alternative. Whatever number lands first is the one the price gets judged against.

### Decoy Effect
A third, clearly worse option makes one of the original two look obvious.
**In an ad:** rarely the ad's job, usually the page's. Flag it as a post-click recommendation rather than trying to build a three-option comparison into thirty seconds.

### Framing Effect
The same fact presented differently is perceived differently.
**In an ad:** "keeps 90% of people out of the clinic" and "10% still need the clinic" are the same fact. Pick the frame that is both true and useful, and check it does not become a claim you cannot substantiate (canon §8).

### Contrast Effect
Things are judged against whatever sits next to them.
**In an ad:** the before state, shown plainly, is what makes the after state legible. It is the whole engine behind problem-visual and before/after construction, and the reason the four visual types include the problem (canon §4).
