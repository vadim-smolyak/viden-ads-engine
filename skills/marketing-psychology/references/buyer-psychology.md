# Buyer psychology (22)

How buyers actually think, decide and misjudge. The most-used file for creative work: most winning ads run one model from here plus one lever from `persuasion.md`.

### Fundamental Attribution Error
People explain others' behaviour by character and their own by circumstance.
**In an ad:** absolve the viewer. "It was never your discipline, it was the routine" outperforms any line that implies they failed.

### Mere Exposure Effect
Familiarity breeds liking. Things seen before feel safer.
**In an ad:** repetition inside the ad builds belief `[T p.44]`, and repetition across the account builds preference. It is also the reason frequency is not automatically fatigue (`shared/evidence.md`).

### Availability Heuristic
People judge likelihood by how easily an example comes to mind. Vivid beats common.
**In an ad:** one concrete, specific scene beats a category-level claim. A vivid demonstration makes the outcome feel probable, not just possible.

### Confirmation Bias
People seek what confirms what they already believe and discount the rest.
**In an ad:** start from what they already believe and redirect it. Head-on contradiction of a held belief loses; a reframe that keeps their belief intact and changes its conclusion wins.

### The Lindy Effect
The longer something has survived, the longer it is likely to last.
**In an ad:** age is a proof asset. "Made the same way since 1974" answers a durability objection that no spec sheet can.

### Mimetic Desire
People want things because other people want them. Desire is socially contagious.
**In an ad:** show the product being wanted, not just used. Waitlists, sold-out signals and visible enthusiasm work through this.

### Sunk Cost Fallacy
Past investment keeps people committed past the point of sense.
**In an ad:** name what they have already sunk into the alternative — the years of appointments, the shelf of half-used products — to make switching feel like stopping a loss rather than starting a risk.

### Endowment Effect
People value a thing more once it is theirs.
**In an ad:** language of possession before purchase. "Your first box", "your plan", "your fit" is not a flourish; it is this model.

### IKEA Effect
Effort invested raises perceived value.
**In an ad:** a quiz, a configurator, a fit-finder or a customisation step makes the result feel more theirs and more valuable.

### Zero-Price Effect
Free is not a low price; it is a different category of decision.
**In an ad:** free shipping, a free sample or a free first month pulls disproportionately harder than an equivalent discount. Lead with the free thing, not the saving.

### Hyperbolic Discounting / Present Bias
Immediate rewards beat larger delayed ones.
**In an ad:** put the first payoff in the near term. "Softer within a week" outperforms "transformed in six months", and it is why speed-of-satisfaction is one of the ways to reinforce desire `[T p.53]`.

### Status-Quo Bias
The current state wins by default because change costs effort and feels risky.
**In an ad:** show the switch, not just the destination. Make the transition visibly small.

### Default Effect
Pre-selected options get accepted.
**In an ad:** recommend one variant explicitly rather than presenting a range. "Start with the medium" removes the decision the ad just created.

### Paradox of Choice
Too many options paralyse.
**In an ad:** one product, one variant, one action. This is the commercial version of one-desire-one-avatar (canon §8).

### Goal-Gradient Effect
Effort accelerates as a goal comes into view.
**In an ad:** show proximity to the outcome, not distance from the problem. "Two steps left" pulls harder than "here is the process".

### Peak-End Rule
Experiences are judged by their peak moment and their ending, not their average.
**In an ad:** build one peak and land the ending. It is also why a weak second half is fixable rather than fatal — attention is re-winnable (`shared/evidence.md`).

### Zeigarnik Effect
Unfinished business occupies the mind. Open loops create tension.
**In an ad:** the curiosity requirement in the hook gate (canon §1) runs on this. Open a loop the ad or the page then closes; leaving it open is a bait, not a hook.

### Pratfall Effect
A competent thing becomes more likeable when it admits a small flaw.
**In an ad:** "we are not the cheapest", "it takes ten minutes to set up" buys credibility for the claims around it, and pre-empts the objection.

### Curse of Knowledge
Once you know something you cannot imagine not knowing it, so experts explain badly.
**In an ad:** the founder's explanation is almost always too advanced. Test the first line on someone outside the category. It is why "too complicated" is one of the three reasons people do not buy `[T p.79]`.

### Mental Accounting
People treat money differently depending on where it came from and what it is for.
**In an ad:** move the cost into a favourable account. Compare a subscription to a habit, not to a rival subscription.

### Regret Aversion
People avoid actions that could lead to regret, even when the odds are good.
**In an ad:** answer regret directly. Guarantees, returns and "no commitment" are not offer decoration, they remove the specific fear that stops the click.

### Bandwagon Effect / Social Proof
People follow what others do; popularity signals both quality and safety.
**In an ad:** counts, review quotes, "most-bought", visible crowds. Strongest when the proof comes from people the viewer recognises as their own — which is why the actor archetypes matter `[T p.63]`.
