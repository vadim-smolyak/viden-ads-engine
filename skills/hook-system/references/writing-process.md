# The Hook Writing Process — the deep path

The book's actual method, plus the three imported ideas that have no equivalent in it. Load this for a deep-path run, a hook workshop, a diagnosis of why a hook is not working, or a swipe-file session. Not needed for "write me ten hooks".

Canon §1's two pre-hook gates apply before any of this: **is the desire proven and large**, and **is the audience unambiguous**. Both fail silently and both invalidate perfect craft.

---

## Step 1 — Write down three things `[T p.33]`

Before a single line:

1. **My customer's desire.**
2. **How many products they have tried before mine** — and why each failed.
3. **Who my customer is.**

That is the whole input list. If any of the three is missing, the gap is in `market-research` or `positioning-mechanisms`, not in the hook.

The second one does the most work and is skipped the most often. A market that has tried nothing needs a claim; a market that has tried four things and failed needs hope restored and every prior failure named `[T p.25]`. It also sets the awareness stage, which sets the script flow (canon §2).

**Worked example** `[T p.33]` — a Facebook-ads offer. Desire: consistent results. Tried before: various strategies, little success. Customer: a Shopify owner spending at least $100/day.

## Step 2 — Positioning hands off three drafts `[T p.34]`

`positioning-mechanisms` chose one of three plays. Each play produces a different first draft from the same three inputs — **write all three, then pick**, because comparing them is cheaper than defending the first one.

| Positioning play | The draft it produces | Book's example `[T p.34]` |
|---|---|---|
| **Same mechanism but better** | Claim the outcome directly, on the axis the market already understands | *"Facebook ads that convert"* |
| **A new mechanism** | Name the vehicle and attach it to the outcome | *"How to use emotion baiting to get consistent results"* |
| **Call out an identity** | Name who it is for, before anything else | *"Facebook Ads for Shopify store owners"* |

Drafts are not meant to be good `[T p.35]`. This is the handoff point where positioning becomes copy, and the only failure here is producing one draft instead of three.

## Step 3 — Escalate curiosity: one claim, five variants `[T p.35]`

Take one base claim and re-enter it five ways. Same promise, different door.

| Move | Shape |
|---|---|
| Time-boxed reveal | *"In 60 seconds, I'll show you how…"* |
| Direct instruction | *"Here's how you can get…"* |
| Enumerated | *"3 ways to get more…"* |
| Superlative route | *"The easiest way to get…"* |
| Consequence-first | *"This will get you more…"* |

Then run the same base claim through the 13 claim patterns — the table with an example shape for each is in `tactics.md`, the five non-duplicate definitions in `tactics-claim-patterns.md`: size, speed, newness, limitation removal, rival comparison. One claim reliably yields a dozen hooks with no new idea, which is what makes volume achievable in Step 5.

## Step 4 — Score the finished hook against six things `[T p.35]`

A finished hook hits all six:

1. Calls out the ideal audience
2. Speaks to identity
3. Speaks to the products they have tried before
4. Implies a benefit
5. Intrigues curiosity
6. Speaks a desire

Items 1, 4 and 5 are canon §1's three golden rules — the pass/fail gate, non-negotiable. Items 2, 3 and 6 are the quality tier above the gate: a hook can pass the gate and still be generic, and these three are what makes it specific to one person. Score every hook on all six on the deep path; score the three on every hook, always.

## Step 5 — Volume discipline `[T p.36]`

> **Write three pages of hooks and discard the first two.** The last page holds the usable ones.

And: **expect your first 100 hooks to be bad.** Both statements are about the same mechanism — the first hooks out are the obvious ones, and obvious hooks are what the competitor already ran.

Operationally, for a deep-path run: generate at roughly 3× the requested count, cut everything that repeats a trigger or a claim shape, then score the survivors. Never present page one. The discipline is the point — presenting the first ten hooks is presenting the least differentiated ten.

**This is also the only defensible reading of the "80% of my time on the hook" line** `[T p.32]`. It is a statement about where the author's *writing hours* go, and the volume rule is what those hours buy. It is **not** a claim about how much of an ad's performance the hook accounts for — that version is folklore with no traceable source (`shared/evidence.md`), and the book's own $1,000 ad had a hook that passed every rule and failed anyway `[T p.65]`.

## Step 6 — Feed the swipe file `[T p.36–37]`

The swipe file is a document you add to over time, and it is the reason a hook session ever starts from something rather than nothing.

**Sources** `[T p.36]`, in the order they pay off:

| Source | Look for |
|---|---|
| YouTube video titles | Structures optimised against a click-through metric for years |
| TikToks | Native phrasing and current containers |
| Instagram Reels | Same, with a more polished register |
| Quora article headlines | Question framings written in the reader's own words |
| News headlines | Compression — the most information per word available anywhere |

Take a hook that provably performed, then **rewrite the structure onto three unrelated products.** If the structure survives all three, it is a pattern; if it only works on the original, it was the subject matter doing the work.

**The book's worked example** uses a Medicare hook `[T p.36]` ported to Facebook ads, coffee and pre-workout. Keep the exercise, replace the example: that specific hook implies knowledge of the reader's financial and health situation and today sits in `FINANCIAL_PRODUCTS_SERVICES`. Excellent teaching case, **never ship it** (`shared/evidence.md`). A safe equivalent structure: *"Little-known [category] detail that could cost you [specific loss]"* → run it on three products and see which survives.

Anything that survives goes into `voice-patterns.md` with all five fields filled.

## Step 7 — Check the calendar before you check the creative `[T p.38]`

Hooks change with the season, and the seasonal hook is often the highest-performing asset in the account **and** the one with the shortest life. Gifting: *"Give your sweetheart a unique gift this Valentine's Day."* Summer: *"Get hair-free skin this summer."*

Canon §8 states the doctrine — desire has a clock. What that means at the hook layer:

- **The Valentine's case** `[T p.38]`: $70k+/day collapsing to $1k/day seven days out. The hook never changed and never got worse. **What expired was the deliverability of the promise it made** — shipping could no longer arrive in time. The hook-level fix is not a rewrite for quality but a rewrite off the dated promise: move the same product onto a desire with no delivery deadline, or onto the next occasion. Diagnosing this as creative fatigue costs a week and produces nothing.
- **The hoodie case** `[T p.38]`: a USA hoodie brand signed in March at roughly $450k/month, two months old. Within 30 days buyers had moved to summer clothing, there was no summer stock, and the warehouse was full of hoodies. Revenue went to zero. **No hook could have been written to fix this** — the desire the copy called out had left the market, and the product could not follow it.

The operational rule: before writing a seasonal hook, confirm the fulfilment window and the last-order date, and write the expiry date next to the hook. Before diagnosing a seasonal collapse, check the calendar and the warehouse first — that is a fulfilment and demand diagnosis, and no amount of rewriting substitutes for it.

# Three imported ideas

Not from the book. Kept because nothing in the book covers the same ground.

## The three layers of every hook

A hook is not a sentence — it is three layers firing at once. Use as a construction frame when writing and a diagnostic frame when something is not working.

**Layer 1 — Visual.** *Why did this make me stop?* The first frame, before a word registers. In static, the image itself. Ask: what stops a thumb before any audio or text lands? Strong ones: unexpected action, native or deliberately unpolished first frame, direct eye contact, the product doing something surprising, texture and movement. Per `shared/evidence.md`, front-load something **recognisable**, not merely arresting — a brand cue landing in the first two seconds is what carries recall.

**Layer 2 — Audio and textual.** *How do sound and text change the visual?* The spoken line, the sound design, the overlay. Test each independently: muted, does the hook still work? Overlay removed, does it still work? Every element earns its place or comes out. The spoken first line should stand alone as a written hook.

**Layer 3 — Psychological.** *What is making me want the next second?* The trigger underneath (`triggers.md`), who is being called out, which loop is open.

**The diagnostic use is the valuable one:** name which layer is carrying the ad. That tells you what to protect when iterating — if the spoken line is doing the work, iterate the visual and leave the line alone. Getting this backwards is how a winner gets iterated to death. Iteration variables are canon §6.

## Hook slop

**A shocking visual disconnected from the product is hook slop.** A makeup brand opening on a gunshot gets the stop and loses the viewer one second later, because the viewer now knows they were tricked and the drop-off is immediate.

The test: **if you have to mentally stretch to justify the connection between the opener and the product, it is slop.** Not "is it arresting" — arresting is easy and cheap. The opener has to be the first beat of the story the ad actually tells.

Same failure in words rather than pictures: an unrelated shock line, a curiosity gap the ad never closes, a joke with no route back to the product truth (the Humor entry in `tactics-d-h.md`). Slop is the most common way a hook passes canon §1 on paper and fails in the feed — it drives curiosity about *something*, just not about the thing being sold.

## Fit in, then stand out

Before writing, ask what the native feed of *this* audience actually looks like — aesthetic, tone, pacing, format.

The hook has to **fit into that feed first**, so it reads as content rather than as an ad, and *then* interrupt inside it. This is why one hook concept performs differently across placements: the container is different even when the psychology is identical.

**Practical split:** the visual layer fits in; the text and psychological layers stand out.

| Placement | The native container | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| **Meta feed** (default) | Mixed, tolerant of production polish, text-heavy captions read | Fewest constraints. Write for it first |
| **TikTok** | Talking-head, unpolished, fast verbal open, on-screen text in platform style | Front-load the spoken line; the first words matter more than the first frame |
| **Reels** | More polished than TikTok, music-led, visual-first | Front-load the visual; the line can arrive a beat later |

Keep hook text high and central on TikTok and Reels — UI chrome eats the lower third and the right edge. **Safe-zone margins are not verified in this plugin** (`shared/evidence.md`); check placement in the platform preview rather than trusting a remembered number.
