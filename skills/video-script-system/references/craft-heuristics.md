# Craft heuristics — with worked before/after pairs

Source: `[T p.67–74]`. These apply during every pass, not as a separate step.

---

## Focus — the most common failure in the book

> "People typically remember only one thing from your ad. That's why focus and simplicity are your greatest assets." `[T p.69]`

The three failure modes `[T p.69]`: too many desires, too many problems, too many customer avatars.

**Rule: one desire or problem, one customer avatar.**

**Bad** `[T p.69]` — coffee alternative, scattered across gut health, energy, taste, caffeine and productivity:

> Coffee ran my life for decades / My gut was always on fire / The more I drinked the less energy I had / I had a serious problem / I found a healthy & yummy coffee alternative / The switch was easy because it tasted just like coffee / It had zero caffeine / Just one cup of this / Gives me long lasting energy, mental clarity, and better gut health than coffee. / Ditch the coffee for something 10x better and tastier.

**Good** `[T p.70]` — one thread, held all the way through:

> The more coffee I had the more tired I felt until I discovered this / As someone who loves coffee / I often feel more tired after having that 2nd and 3rd cup / Which really killed my productivity at work / A friend of mine told me to try mushroom elixir / On day 1, I noticed I didn't need that 3rd cup of coffee / And my energy has been more stable throughout the day / All I had done was add 1 scoop of mushroom elixir to my coffee / 30 days later and I've never been more productive at work / Without having to reach for that second cup

Note what the good version *drops*: gut health, taste, caffeine content, "10x better." All true, all on-brand, all cut.

## Emotional vs logical — alternate, don't cluster

> "Your writing should strike an equal balance between emotional and logical statements. A well-balanced script flows by alternating between the two." `[T p.67]`

| | Focus | Example `[T p.67]` |
|---|---|---|
| **Emotional** | How happy the prospect will be | "Imagine being able to confidently write Facebook ads that scale" |
| **Logical** | How the product works | "The exact process that Nick Theriot followed to generate $100 million in revenue for his clients" |

**Failure modes, both directions** `[T p.67]`:
- Too many logical statements up front → you bore the prospect.
- Too many emotional statements up front → the prospect loses belief.

Practical check: mark each line E or L and look at the pattern. Runs of three or more of either need breaking up.

## Dark side vs bright side

> "Don't focus too much on the problem or symptoms your audience is currently facing. Instead, sell them on the new reality your product offers compared to their current situation." `[T p.67]`

**Bad** `[T p.67]` — wallows, and the emotional escalation goes nowhere useful:

> Do you struggle to scale Facebook ads? / If so, you're just like me. / Spending hours watching YouTube videos. / Learning strategy after strategy. / But still no success. / It often felt like I was running out of hope. / What would my family think of me if I never figured this out? / Would I be a failure the rest of my life? / I knew I had to find a way to make this work. / I then stumbled upon a new strategy…

**Good** `[T p.68]` — states the pain once, then spends its lines on the new reality with specifics and speed:

> Do you struggle to scale Facebook Ads? / After wasting tons of time trying to scale using cost caps and bid caps. / I found this new strategy that took my ad account from spending $1,000 to $10,000 per day / In literally just 10 days!! / I've finally been able to find a strategy that gives me confidence running ads. / And the best part? / It only takes less than an hour a day to do. / This strategy is called…

The distinction is *proportion*, not avoidance. One line of pain earns the transformation. Six lines of pain is a different ad, and a worse one.

## Desires vs benefits vs features

The chain runs **feature → benefit → desire** `[T p.71]`.

Worked example — Shopify dropshipping course:

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| Feature | Step-by-step blueprint on launching your dropshipping store |
| Benefit | Make an extra $1,000/month |
| Desire | Make more money |

Two questions, in this order `[T p.71]`:
1. What is the benefit of this feature?
2. What desire does this benefit appeal to?

**Then only surface benefits and features serving that one desire.** Everything else, however true, is dilution.

## Clever vs simple

> "Simple, straightforward copy always outperforms clever copy. While clever copy might boost your ego, simple copy will boost your bank account." `[T p.72]`

The mechanism: "Clever writing forces people to think, as if solving a puzzle" `[T p.72]`. A scroller escaping their day will not solve your puzzle.

**Clever** `[T p.72]` — the book's deliberate anti-example:

> Coffee used to rule my life, but now I'm high on... mushrooms? / My friend slipped me this "secret elixir." Totally legal, he winked. / Just one scoop and suddenly I'm working like I'm possessed by a caffeinated ghost! / Day 1: Who needs sleep when you've got fungus juice? / Day 30: Haven't blinked in weeks. Is this what enlightenment feels like? / …

**Simple** — the same product, the focused version above `[T p.73]`.

Signs a line is being clever: wordplay, a joke that needs the previous line, an ironic aside, a reference the reader must recognise, or anything you'd describe as "fun."

## How long should an ad be?

> "This is an age-old question with no straightforward answer—it depends." `[T p.74]`

Two competing risks `[T p.74]`: a long ad struggles to hold attention; a short ad delivers the prospect to the site under-informed.

**Length is a function of product complexity × stage of buying mindset.** The book's steak analogy `[T p.74]`:

- **Hungry prospect:** show the steak, say how to buy it. Done.
- **Prospect who just ate five courses:** now you need the story — rarest in the world, sourced from a small town in Japan, the cow costs ten times a regular American cow, rich flavour that melts in your mouth, it's Wagyu, we rarely have it in stock, tonight I have one left, we're the only restaurant in the city that carries it, and I don't know when we'll get more. Would you like to try one?

Same product. Different mindset. Different length.

**2026 note.** The book's refusal to name a number is vindicated. Kantar (5 Feb 2026, control-exposed, fielded 19 Dec 2025 – 8 Jan 2026, **sample size not disclosed**) found "15 second ads can pack just as much brand building punch," while a 30-second cut pulled **1.4× higher active attention** in an NFL environment on Amazon Prime. Kantar (29 Jul 2024) found a 15s cut beat a 6s cut where the idea needed setup. The defensible reading is "shorter is *sufficient*," not "shorter is better." Placement-specific optimal runtimes could not be verified — see `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/shared/evidence.md`.

## Entertainment plus selling power

> "All ads need to have a balance of entertainment as well as selling power." `[T p.39]`

The reason is the viewer's state `[T p.39]`: people scroll when they're bored in a queue, on a lunch break, home on the couch after work, "full of anxiety and need an escape." An ad with no entertainment value gets swiped regardless of how good the argument is.

This is also why over-educating fails — see `campaign-troubleshooting` for the diagnostic.

## Repetition builds belief

> "Repetition is your most powerful tool. By consistently showcasing how your product delivers what your customer wants, you effectively hypnotize them into buying." `[T p.44]`

This is what pass 2 operationalises. The constraint that stops it becoming monotony is Focus: repeat the *same desire* from *different angles*, never a different desire each time.
